### Do I have to moderate chat?

Yes.

No-chat operation should not be presented as a realistic option for Twitch and YouTube. If chat is enabled on those platforms, moderation is often required by the platform.

Unattended chat creates risks for the community, for staff, and for compliance with platform rules. This is especially true in conservation where unhelpful and inaccurate conversation about the animals and their care can take off.

Moderation is not only about removing obvious abuse. It also includes:

* keeping the tone workable for ordinary viewers
* preventing speculation and rumor from spiraling
* redirecting invasive questions about staff, locations, or operations
* stopping unsafe suggestions or harassment before they become the dominant tone of chat

## Minimum moderation practice

At minimum, a live channel should have:

* published chat rules
* at least one person responsible for moderation when chat is active
* a way to escalate issues to staff
* clear boundaries on what moderators can decide on their own
* a willingness to time out, remove, or hide users when needed

Good moderation does not need to feel heavy-handed, but it does need to be present.

The practical baseline is simple:

* post rules where viewers can see them
* make sure moderators know the tone you want
* act early on harassment, hate, spam, and invasive behavior
* do not let harmful behavior sit in chat unanswered

For conservation streams in particular, moderators may also need guidance on recurring situations such as:

* viewers demanding private information about staff or sites
* viewers pushing for medical details that should stay private
* viewers turning uncertain observations into confident claims about welfare problems
* viewers arguing over animal behavior in ways that become hostile or misleading

Moderators are there to keep the space useful and safe, not to win arguments.

## Platform-specific moderation notes

These summaries are useful as platform-specific context and should not be read as a substitute for checking the live platform documentation.

### Twitch

Twitch frames moderation and safety as part of the creator's responsibility. Creator Camp says that keeping the channel experience positive should always be a priority and points streamers to Terms of Service, Community Guidelines, reporting, enforcement, and appeals as part of normal channel operation.

If a conservation organization enables Twitch chat, it should assume it is responsible for enforcing channel rules and responding to harmful behavior promptly.

### YouTube

YouTube gives creators direct tools to moderate both comments and live chat, including moderator and managing moderator roles for live chat.

YouTube says moderators can remove content, put users on timeout from 10 seconds up to 24 hours, and hide users from the channel. Those tools make it possible to respond quickly, but they still need people using them.

Strong moderation tools are a reason to actively manage chat, not a reason to leave it unattended.

## What fundraising opportunities are there?

Fundraising options fall into a few broad categories:

* charity and nonprofit-specific mechanisms
* recurring support such as subscriptions and memberships
* live fan-funding tools such as Bits and Super Chat
* sponsorships

These are not all the same thing, and it helps to keep them separate.

Charity tools are built to raise money for a nonprofit. Subscriptions, memberships, Bits, and Super Chat are platform support tools that usually support the channel itself. Sponsorships are a separate relationship again.

That distinction matters because viewers will often assume all on-stream money works the same way unless you explain it clearly.

## Platform-specific fundraising notes

These summaries distinguish the main platform-specific options and should be checked against current platform documentation.

### YouTube charity and nonprofit tools

YouTube Giving is YouTube's nonprofit fundraising product. Eligible creators can add a donate button to videos and live streams, and viewers can donate directly on the watch page or through live chat.

YouTube says eligible channels generally must be in the YouTube Partner Program, have at least 10,000 subscribers, be based in an eligible location, and not be marked `made for kids`.

YouTube also says:

* `Super Chat` and `Super Stickers` are turned off on live streams with donations to avoid confusion
* ads are not affected by adding a fundraiser
* creators cannot run members-only live chat with the donate button active
* community fundraisers allow multiple creators to collaborate on the same cause
* 100% of donations go to the nonprofit and YouTube covers transaction fees

One limitation is that the nonprofit must be an eligible US-registered `501(c)(3)` public charity, or a qualifying US sister organization.

For organizations that qualify, this is the clearest platform-native option when the goal is to raise money for a nonprofit rather than for the channel itself.

### Twitch charity tools

Twitch charity-style fundraising should be treated separately from regular monetization tools such as subscriptions, Bits, ads, and sponsorships.

Twitch distinguishes standard monetization from charity fundraising, but this section should be checked against Twitch's current charity documentation before publication.

For now, keep the guidance limited:

* keep Twitch charity fundraising separate from normal channel monetization in your planning and messaging
* verify current Twitch Charity eligibility, supported nonprofits, fees, and regional availability from Twitch's live help documentation before publication or launch

### Twitch subscriptions and Bits

Subscriptions are Twitch's core recurring support model, with recurring or one-time support, tiered options, and giftable support. Creator Camp also notes that Prime Gaming includes one free monthly channel subscription for eligible users.

Bits are a purchasable virtual good used to Cheer in chat. Twitch describes them as a way for viewers to show support, celebrate moments, and amplify messages.

Twitch also says selling, trading, bartering, transferring Bits for currency, or using Bits to bet or wager is prohibited.

These are platform-specific support tools, not a substitute for charity fundraising.

If you use them, be plain with viewers about where the money goes: support for the channel is not the same thing as a donation to a nonprofit.

### YouTube memberships and Super Chat

Channel memberships are YouTube's recurring support model.

Super Chat and Super Stickers are live fan-funding tools that let viewers purchase highlighted messages or stickers in live chat. YouTube says they are moderated the same way as live chat, and that creators receive 70% of confirmed Supers revenue after local sales tax and iOS App Store fees are deducted.

These are platform-specific support tools. They should be understood separately from nonprofit fundraising products such as YouTube Giving.

### Sponsorships

Sponsorships are a separate category rather than a platform-native donation tool.

Twitch Creator Camp describes common sponsorship formats such as product placements, sponsored gameplay, streamer-read ads, in-kind sponsorships, and endorsements. It also says branded content on Twitch must be disclosed with Twitch's branded content tool and must comply with applicable laws and platform rules.

For conservation organizations, the practical question is fit. A sponsorship should make sense for the mission, the audience, and the trust placed in the stream. If it does not, it is usually not worth the trade.

## How do I interact with the community?

Community interaction can range from simple chat engagement to full day-in-the-life or educational streams. This is best approached as a range rather than a single ideal format.

That range includes:

* [full "Animal care chats" style streams](https://youtu.be/9zSCjuYKXC8)
* [voice-over "chat chats" style streams](https://youtu.be/HfdYbfm1s2c)
* interaction via chat
* moderation only

Basic chat interaction is the lightest-touch option. More involved streams may support more day-to-day connection and education. The useful question is not whether you are doing community "enough." It is what level of interaction your team can sustain well.

Some organizations will want regular hosted streams with staff on camera. Others may mainly run 24/7 live cams and show up in chat when there is something to explain, answer, or celebrate. Both can work.

### Full "Animal care chats" style streams

This is the most involved end of the spectrum. Staff are actively present and the stream becomes a direct education and relationship-building space.

This can work well when:

* staff are comfortable speaking live
* there is a clear educational angle
* viewers are likely to have recurring questions about care, conservation, or the animals' routines

It can create strong connection, but it also asks more of the team. It is harder to do well if staff are already stretched.

### Voice-over "chat chats" style streams

This can be a good middle ground. The audience still gets context, explanation, and a sense of presence, but the format may be easier to run than a fully on-camera stream. This is also perfect for guests, experts or staff that aren't on-site - it can be done at a desk at home or at an office!

### Interaction via chat

Basic chat interaction is the lightest-touch community format.

That may mean answering questions when staff are available, posting occasional context about what viewers are seeing, or acknowledging important moments without running a separate hosted segment.

This can be enough. A stream does not need constant talking to feel cared for.

### Moderated only

Some streams will mostly rely on moderators and community norms rather than frequent staff participation. See the platform specific minimum moderation requirements for what this looks like.
